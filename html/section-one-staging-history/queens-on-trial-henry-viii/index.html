Queens on trial: Henry VIII

Though less familiar today, Henry VIII; or, All Is True was once among Shakespeare's most performed plays.

Co-written with John Fletcher, Henry VIII covers the rise of Anne Boleyn and the King's divorce from Katherine of Aragon. 'Queen Katherine', who is put on trial in Act 2, became a favourite tragic role for actresses such as Sarah Siddons (1755-1831) and Ellen Terry (1847-1928).

From at least 1727, when a revival by Colley Cibber made explicit reference to George II's coronation, Henry VIII was a popular reference point for contemporary royal events. The play's interest in royal women, and in sexuality, made it ripe for comparison with troubled latter-day royal marriages. Meanwhile, its English Reformation backdrop lent it an edge of anti-Catholicism. This made it a useful tool for criticising overreaching by members of the Hanoverian dynasty, who had been installed in Britain primarily because of their Protestant faith.

Shakespeare and Fletcher's Henry VIII

Shakespeare collaborated with his fellow playwright John Fletcher on this history play towards the end of his career.

It focuses on the events surrounding Henry VIII’s contested divorce from his first wife, Katherine of Aragon, and wedding to his second, Anne Boleyn (or, as the play calls her, Anna Bullen).

But the central character is the Machiavellian Cardinal Wolsey, whose rise and downfall structure its plot. The subtitle All is True makes a claim for the play’s historical accuracy.  

It was a performance of Henry VIII that led to the Globe theatre burning down in 1613. However, it has not been tainted with superstition like the earlier Jacobean play, Macbeth.

During the eighteenth century, the play was enormously popular—its major set pieces allowed for spectacular staging, particularly in the trial scene where Katherine confronts Wolsey, and during the coronation of Anne Boleyn. The play was often staged to mark real-life coronations, including that of George II in 1727.
