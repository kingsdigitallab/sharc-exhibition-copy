Family trees: The Merry Wives of Windsor

The Merry Wives of Windsor has special royal connections. Set in and around Windsor Castle, it was once said to have been commissioned by Elizabeth I herself.

Historically, some royals were put off by the plot's bawdy elements. However, its local setting and vision of provincial English life - encapsulated in the character of 'sweet Anne Page' - gave it an enduring appeal.

Much royal interest focused on the identity of 'Herne's Oak', the haunted tree in Windsor Home Park that forms the backdrop for the play's final scenes. In 1863, when a possible 'Herne's Oak' blew down, Queen Victoria commissioned a series of items from its wood. In the process, she created a Windsor equivalent to the trade in relics from ‘Shakespeare’s mulberry tree’ in Stratford-upon-Avon, first established in the eighteenth century.

The Merry Wives of Windsor, and ‘Herne’s Oak’ with it, therefore inserted both the royal family, and Windsor Castle, into the popular conception of the national poet – and England's physical landscape.

Shakespeare's The Merry Wives of Windsor

The fat, drunken knight Sir John Falstaff appears in Shakespeare’s Henry IV plays as a companion to the Prince of Wales; at the end of Henry IV part 2, the prince rejects his former drinking companion upon becoming king.

Where The Merry Wives of Windsor fits into this narrative is disputed. The play finds Falstaff pursuing two middle-class married women, Mistress Ford and Mistress Page, in prosperous Windsor. The women decide to humiliate both Falstaff, for his presumption, and their husbands, for their mistrust, and orchestrate a series of practical jokes.

Meanwhile, Mistress Page’s daughter Anne is courted by several ludicrous suitors, and ultimately rejects all of them to marry her own preferred candidate, Master Fenton. 

The play’s final scene is set at Herne’s Oak, a haunted tree in Windsor Great Park which became the focus of royal interest over the subsequent centuries. An apocryphal story originating in the early eighteenth century suggested that the play itself was a royal commission, inspired by Elizabeth I’s request to see “Falstaff in love.”
