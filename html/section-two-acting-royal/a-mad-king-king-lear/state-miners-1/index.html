State Miners

George Cruikshank

Date:1811

Hand-coloured etching

British Museum 1868,0808.12616

Image credit: © The Trustees of the British Museum. Shared under a Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial-ShareAlike 4.0 International (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0) licence.

Guided tour

Opportunists freely plunder from the chaotic national Treasury following the second - ultimately final - deterioration of George III’s health, and the resultant power struggles in government.

Like many satires, this print is ruthlessly critical of the government: the Prime Minister Spencer Perceval shovels gold into a pair of trousers, while various other political figures appear as crude, greedy caricatures.

However, Cruikshank is unusual in acknowledging the apparent trigger for the king’s decline: the recent death of his favourite daughter, Princess Amelia. A painting of Lear mourning the dead Cordelia shows the king exclaiming George’s famous ‘what what’. Even so, Cruikshank is careful to keep the comment to the print’s compositional sidelines.

Shakespeare in satire

Prospero of the Enchanted Isle

After G.L.

Date:1798

Hand-coloured etching

24.8 x 30.0 cm

British Museum, 1948,0214.987

Image credit: © The Trustees of the British Museum. Shared under a Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial-ShareAlike 4.0 International (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0) licence.

'Prospero of the Enchanted Isle' shows how familiar characters and situations from Shakespeare's plays could be adapted to comment on contemporary affairs. The audience for satirical prints overlapped heavily with the theatre-going public, so satirists could expect those who viewed and purchased the prints to pick up Shakespearean references.

In this example, George III, as The Tempest’s magician-ruler, Prospero, defends England – here, Shakespeare’s ‘Enchanted Isle’ – from the bestial figure of the French Revolution squatting on the burning city of Paris and speaking Caliban’s claim to ownership of the island. George misquotes Prospero’s most famous speech to threaten the encroaching French ships with dissolution. In contrasting sunny England, where the king is backed up with ‘huzzas’, with the chaos of smoky France, ‘G. L.’ uses Shakespeare as a tool to establish and underline national difference.
